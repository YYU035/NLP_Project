  1. OBJECTIVES To determine the extent to which type of hospital admission (emergency compared with elective) and surgical procedure varied by socioeconomic circumstances, age, sex, and year of admission for colorectal, breast, and lung cancer.
  2. DESIGN Repeated cross sectional study with data from individual patients, 1 April 1999 to 31 March 2006.
  3. SETTING Hospital episode statistics (HES) dataset.
  4. PARTICIPANTS 564 821 patients aged 50 and over admitted with a diagnosis of colorectal, breast, or lung cancer.
  5. MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES Proportion of patients admitted as emergencies, and the proportion receiving the recommended surgical treatment.
  6. RESULTS Patients from deprived areas, older people, and women were more likely to be admitted as emergencies.
  7. For example, the adjusted odds ratio for patients with breast cancer in the least compared with most deprived fifth of deprivation was 0.63 (95% confidence interval 0.60 to 0.66) and the adjusted odds ratio for patients with lung cancer aged 80-89 compared with those aged 50-59 was 3.13 (2.93 to 3.34).
  8. There were some improvements in disparities between age groups but not for patients living in deprived areas over time.
  9. Patients from deprived areas were less likely to receive preferred procedures for rectal, breast, and lung cancer.
  10. These findings did not improve with time.
  11. For example, 67.4% (3529/5237) of patients in the most deprived fifth of deprivation had anterior resection for rectal cancer compared with 75.5% (4497/5959) of patients in the least deprived fifth (1.34, 1.22 to 1.47).
  12. Over half (54.0%, 11 256/20 849) of patients in the most deprived fifth of deprivation had breast conserving surgery compared with 63.7% (18 445/28 960) of patients in the least deprived fifth (1.21, 1.16 to 1.26).
  13. Men were less likely than women to undergo anterior resection and lung cancer resection and older people were less likely to receive breast conserving surgery and lung cancer resection.
  14. For example, the adjusted odds ratio for lung cancer patients aged 80-89 compared with those aged 50-59 was 0.52 (0.46 to 0.59).
  15. Conclusions Despite the implementation of the NHS Cancer Plan, social factors still strongly influence access to and the provision of care.

Corpus document 5641851: Social and geographical factors affecting access to treatment of colorectal cancer: a cancer registry study

Paper verdict: INSUFFICIENT → INSUFFICIENT. Gate removed all candidate passages.

Original abstract; yellow marks benchmark rationale sentences
  1. OBJECTIVE Cancer outcomes vary between and within countries with patients from deprived backgrounds known to have inferior survival.
  2. The authors set out to explore the effect of deprivation in relation to the accessibility of hospitals offering diagnostic and therapeutic services on stage at presentation and receipt of treatment.
  3. DESIGN Analysis of a Cancer Registry Database.
  4. Data included stage and treatment details from the first 6 months.
  5. The socioeconomic status of the immediate area of residence and the travel time from home to hospital was derived from the postcode.
  6. SETTING Population-based study of patients resident in a large area in the north of England.
  7. PARTICIPANTS 39 619 patients with colorectal cancer diagnosed between 1994 and 2002.
  8. OUTCOMES MEASURED Stage of diagnosis and receipt of treatment in relation to deprivation and distance from hospital.
  9. RESULTS Patients in the most deprived quartile were significantly more likely to be diagnosed at stage 4 for rectal cancer (OR 1.516, p<0.05) but less so for colonic cancer.
  10. There was a trend for both sites for patients in the most deprived quartile to be less likely to receive chemotherapy for stage 4 disease.
  11. Patients with colonic cancer were very significantly less likely to receive any treatment if they came from any but the most affluent area (ORs 0.639, 0.603 and 0.544 in increasingly deprived quartiles), this may have been exacerbated if the hospital was distant from their residence (OR for forth quartile for both travel and deprivation 0.731, not significant).
  12. The effect was less for rectal cancer and no effect of distance was seen.
  13. CONCLUSIONS Residing in a deprived area is associated with tendencies to higher stage at diagnosis and especially in the case of colonic cancer to reduced receipt of treatment.
  14. These observations are consistent with other findings and indicate that access to diagnosis requires further investigation.

Corpus document 5372432: Geographical access to healthcare in Northern England and post-mortem diagnosis of cancer.

Paper verdict: INSUFFICIENT → INSUFFICIENT. Gate removed all candidate passages.

Original abstract; yellow marks benchmark rationale sentences
  1. BACKGROUND There is some previous evidence that diagnosis of cancer at death, recorded as registry death certificate only records, is associated with problems of access to care.
  2. METHODS Records from the Northern and Yorkshire Cancer Registry for patients registered with breast, colorectal, lung, ovarian or prostate cancer between 1994 and 2002 were supplemented with measures of travel time to general practitioner and hospital services, and social deprivation.
  3. Logistic regression was used to identify predictors of records where diagnosis was at death.
  4. RESULTS There was no association between the odds diagnosis at death and access to primary care.
  5. For all sites except breast, the highest odds of being a cancer diagnosed at death fell among those living in the highest quartile of hospital travel time, although it was only statistically significant for colorectal and ovary tumours.
  6. Those in the most deprived and furthest travel time to hospital quartile were 2.6 times more likely to be a diagnosis at death case compared with those in the most affluent and proximal areas.
  7. CONCLUSIONS There is some evidence that poorer geographical access to tertiary care, in particular when coupled with social disadvantages, may be associated with increased odds of diagnosis at death.

Corpus document 14021596: The association of Red cell distribution width and in-hospital mortality in older adults admitted to the emergency department

Paper verdict: INSUFFICIENT → INSUFFICIENT.

Original abstract; yellow marks benchmark rationale sentences
  1. BACKGROUND The objective of the study was to test the hypothesis that elevated red cell distribution width (RDW) at admission increases the risk of mortality in older patients admitted to the emergency department (ED).
  2. METHODS We performed a retrospective analysis of patients admitted to the ED between May 2013 and October 2013.
  3. We included patients who were older than 65 years who visited the ED with any medical problems.
  4. Baseline RDW values were measured at the time of admission to the ED.
  5. The primary outcome was all-cause in-hospital mortality.
  6. Multivariate logistic analysis was performed.
  7. RESULTS A total of 1,990 patients were finally included in this study.
  8. The mean age was 75 years (SD 7), and 936 (47 %) subjects were male.
  9. The in-hospital mortality rate was 3.76 % (74 patients).
  10. RDW values higher in non-survivors than in survivors (15.9 ± 2.5 vs. 13.8 ± 1.7, p < 0.001).
  11. Multivariate logistic analysis showed that RDW was associated with all-cause in-hospital mortality after adjusting for other confounding factors.
  12. DISCUSSION RDW value at admission is an independent predictor of all-cause in-hospital mortality among patients older than 65 years.
  13. After adjustment for multiple confounders, the all-cause in-hospital mortality rate increased by 21.8% for each 1% increase in RDW.
  14. CONCLUSION These results show that RDW at admission is associated with in-hospital mortality among patients older than 65.
  15. Thus, RDW at admission may represent a surrogate marker of disease severity.
  16. We caution against using these findings to aid clinical decision-making process until they are externally validated.

Corpus document 32084655: Time from finding abnormality on mass-screening to final diagnosis of lung cancer.

Paper verdict: INSUFFICIENT → INSUFFICIENT.

Original abstract; yellow marks benchmark rationale sentences
  1. Mass-screening for lung cancer is rather a unique system in Japan.
  2. This study illustrates time from finding abnormality on mass-screening to final diagnosis of lung cancer.
  3. Among the 517 patients with lung cancer who were admitted to our hospital over a 10-year period up to December 2001, 83 (16.1%) were detected by mass-screening.
  4. We reviewed medical records of the 83 patients and determined the intervals from the mass-screening to the pathological diagnosis with clinical staging.
  5. Time from the mass-screening to the date of hospital visit was <2 months in 62 (74.7%) cases.
  6. Five (6.0%) patients visited hospital more than 6 months after the mass-screening.
  7. With respect to the interval, there was no statistical difference in gender (p=0.0680) and age (p=0.1532).
  8. Among 60 patients who were referred from outside, on average, patients visited our hospital 0.5 month after they first sought medical attention at nearby clinic, and at our hospital 0.5 month was required to make a pathological diagnosis of lung cancer with TNM staging.
  9. There was a statistical difference in survival between the patients who were diagnosed <4 months and the patients who were diagnosed >4 months from the screening (p=0.0487).
  10. The interval in most cases was acceptable.
  11. However, further improvements are still needed to minimize the delay and to maximize the benefits of early cancer detection.
